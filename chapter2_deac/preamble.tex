\chapter{Information Specialisation in Learned Representations of Actor-Critic Algorithms}

\section{Preamble}

This chapter investigates how the architecture and learning objectives of actor--critic agents shape the information encoded in their learned representations. In particular, it asks why separating the actor and critic networks often improves sample efficiency and generalisation relative to architectures in which both components share a representation. To study this question, the work introduces mutual-information-based metrics that quantify how much a representation encodes about environment-instance identity, state value, inverse dynamics, and forward dynamics. These quantities provide a common language for characterising the different information requirements of the actor and critic, and for relating representation learning to instance overfitting.

The chapter combines theoretical analysis with an extensive empirical study of on-policy actor--critic algorithms across discrete- and continuous-control benchmarks. It shows that, once their representations are decoupled, the actor and critic systematically specialise in different forms of information. Actor representations preferentially retain information relevant to action selection, whereas critic representations encode substantially more information about value and environmental dynamics. The analysis further establishes an upper bound on deployment generalisation error that depends on the mutual information between the actor representation and the identity of the training environment instance. This result helps explain why decoupling can improve generalisation: it limits the transfer of instance-specific information from the critic's learning objective into the actor representation. The chapter also shows that auxiliary representation-learning objectives are most effective for the actor when they favour instance-invariant information, rather than merely increasing the amount of dynamics or value information encoded.

Finally, the work reveals that the critic affects more than representation learning. Even when actor and critic parameters are fully separated, the critic's value estimates enter the policy-gradient objective and thereby influence the policy's updates, state-visitation distribution, and collected training data. This identifies an additional role for the critic in exploration, while also exposing a residual source of interference between policy optimisation and value learning.

Within the broader thesis, this chapter provides the first intervention on the simulation bottleneck: it diagnoses how agents overfit when trained on a restricted set of simulated environments and identifies architectural and representational mechanisms that can mitigate this failure. The publication presenting this work is reproduced without modification in the remainder of the chapter.
