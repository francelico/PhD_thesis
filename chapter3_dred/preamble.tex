\chapter{Data Regularised Environment Design}

\section{Preamble}

This chapter investigates how the selection and generation of training environments affect zero-shot generalisation in RL. Its first contribution is an information-theoretic account of adaptive level sampling. For actor--critic agents with shared representations, the chapter shows that prioritising levels according to value-prediction loss acts as a form of data regularisation: it limits the mutual information between the agent's internal representation and the identities of the training levels. This connects adaptive sampling to the minimisation of an upper bound on the generalisation gap, providing a theoretical explanation for improvements that had previously been observed primarily through empirical evaluation.

The chapter then examines the limitations of both adaptive sampling and unsupervised environment design. Resampling can reduce overfitting to a fixed training set, but cannot introduce variations absent from that set. Conversely, generating new environments without reference to a target distribution can induce substantial distribution shift, causing an agent to train on challenging but deployment-irrelevant instances. To characterise this failure, the chapter introduces the shift-induced gap as a complement to the conventional generalisation gap. It then proposes Data-Regularised Environment Design (\hbox{DRED}), which combines adaptive sampling with a generative model learned from an initial set of environment parameters. By grounding generated instances in this data, DRED expands the training distribution while preserving its correspondence to the target task. Experiments show that this combination improves zero-shot transfer over fixed-set sampling and prior unsupervised environment-design methods.

Within the broader thesis narrative, this work addresses the second condition of the simulation bottleneck: the requirement to provide agents with a sufficiently diverse and representative collection of training scenarios. It connects instance overfitting to the mechanism that generates the agent's experience and shows that effective environment design must balance increased diversity against distributional fidelity. DRED thereby reduces the manual burden of specifying large training sets, while retaining the interactable simulator assumed by this stage of the thesis. The publication presenting this work is reproduced without modification in the remainder of the chapter.
