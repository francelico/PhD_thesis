%%%% ABSTRACT
\abstract{
Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a powerful framework for learning adaptive control policies from interaction, but its practical deployment remains constrained by what this thesis terms the simulation bottleneck: the reliance on bespoke simulators and sufficiently diverse training scenarios for effective learning and generalisation. In this thesis, we investigate both the causes of this bottleneck and algorithmic strategies for mitigating it. First, we study instance overfitting in deep RL, showing that the representations learned by modern actor-critic algorithms can make agents overly specialised to their training environments and drives poor generalisation. In particular, we demonstrate that separating actor and critic representations promotes useful specialisation, reduces harmful instance-specific encoding, and improves transfer. To support our study, we introduce mutual-information–based metrics that quantify specialisation and instance overfitting in the agents' representations. We show that the generalisation error of agents at deployment admits an upper bound determined by these metrics. %Complementing this analysis, we develop high-throughput simulation benchmarks, \hbox{PixelBrax} and \hbox{MEAL}, that make it possible to study generalisation and adaptation at scale under controlled distributions of training and evaluation instances.

The second part of the thesis develops generative approaches for relaxing, and ultimately bypassing, the simulation bottleneck. We introduce Data-Regularised Environment Design (\hbox{DRED}), a method that augments limited training sets with synthetic but distributionally grounded environment instances generated by a learned model, thereby improving zero-shot transfer without incurring the distribution shift common in prior unsupervised environment design methods. Finally, we move beyond hand-crafted simulators altogether through world models learned from offline interaction data. We present \hbox{PERSIST}, a world modelling framework that maintains a persistent latent 3D representation of the environment and uses it to generate action-conditioned visual experience with improved spatial memory, geometric consistency, and long-horizon stability. Together, these contributions advance a broader research programme aimed at making RL less dependent on manually engineered simulators, and closer to a scalable, data-driven paradigm for learning generalist policies.
}


%%%% LAY SUMMARY
\summary{
Reinforcement learning is a way of training computers to make decisions through trial and
error. Rather than being told exactly what to do, an artificial agent performs actions, observes
their consequences, and gradually learns which behaviours lead to better outcomes. However,
learning through unrestricted interaction in the real world can be slow, expensive, or unsafe.
Reinforcement learning agents are therefore often trained in computer simulations.

This creates what this thesis calls the \emph{simulation bottleneck}. Before an agent can learn,
a suitable simulator must be built, and it must contain enough varied training situations to
prepare the agent for deployment. Constructing these simulators and scenarios can require
substantial human expertise and engineering. The effort avoided by not manually programming
the agent's behaviour can therefore reappear in the design of the world in which it learns.

This thesis addresses the simulation bottleneck at three levels. First, it studies why agents
trained on a limited set of simulated environments often perform poorly in new ones. The results
show that agents can retain details that identify individual training environments instead of
learning information that transfers more broadly.

Second, the thesis introduces Data-Regularised Environment Design, or DRED, which generates
additional training environments from a limited initial dataset. Unlike methods that generate
situations simply because they are novel or difficult, DRED keeps them grounded in the
distribution of environments that the agent is expected to encounter. This expands training
diversity while avoiding unrealistic or irrelevant scenarios, leading to better generalisation.

Finally, the thesis investigates whether the simulator itself can be learned from recorded
experience. It introduces PERSIST, a learned world model that maintains an internal
three-dimensional representation of its environment. This persistent representation allows it
to remember previously visited places and generate more geometrically consistent and stable
visual experiences over long interactions. Together, these contributions reduce the need for
manually designed training scenarios and simulators, moving reinforcement learning towards a
more scalable, data-driven approach.
}
